Green Packaging Doesn't Mean Green Inside

In Slovakia, packaging may not carry the label "eco-friendly" unless the manufacturer can prove it. A Czech amendment gives the same labels two more years of peace. Why do would-be green packages harm us, and how can we avoid being fooled?

Picture two bottles of dish soap. One stands on a drugstore shelf in the Slovak town of Holíč, the other a few hundred meters away, across the bridge over the Morava River, in the Czech town of Hodonín. They share the same green color, the same little leaf on the cap and the same word "gentle" in large letters. On the Slovak side, though, since September 27 that word has to prove something. On the Czech side it is still allowed to just look nice.

Packaging that speaks for the product

🍃 Packaging is the product's clothing. The product cannot speak for itself, so for so-called eco-friendly items the talking is done by green color, a leaf, a drop of water and the word "natural." Green packaging does not just sell us detergent or cosmetics. Above all, it sells us the good feeling that we are buying the right thing.

That feeling is stronger than we usually admit. Experiments have shown that people rate food in "green" packaging as healthier than exactly the same food in ordinary packaging, and this holds even for food they would otherwise not consider healthy.

The green color and the leaf symbol quietly suggest that the purchase will benefit not only nature but our health too. Yet the design of the packaging itself tells us nothing at all about what is inside.

A dress code since September

🧾 The EU directive on empowering consumers for the green transition introduced a strict dress code for packaging, in force across the Union since September 27. General claims such as "green" or "nature-friendly" no longer reach customers without specific, verifiable proof.

A sustainability label that a company designs and awards to itself is now banned unless it rests on independent certification or was set up by the relevant public authority. So if only the cap is made of recycled material, the manufacturer may not create the impression that the whole bottle is.

The reason for this strictness is simple. An earlier investigation by the European Commission found that 53 percent of green claims about sustainable products on the EU market were vague, misleading or unfounded, and that 40 percent had no evidence behind them at all.

At the same time, more than 230 different sustainability labels circulate in the Union, and for roughly half of them verification was weak or missing entirely. Green packaging has thus become the cheapest substitute for real sustainability.

Two years in the wardrobe

⏳ Slovakia passed the necessary implementing law a year earlier than the Czech Republic, and its companies had almost a year to adjust their labels. The Czech Republic, by contrast, missed the deadline. The directive required the rules to be written into national law by March 27. The Czech government, however, sent its bill to the Chamber of Deputies late, and MPs approved it only on October 14, after the date when the rules took effect across the Union.

Late legislation created a problem, which the MPs then tried to solve with a blanket exemption. MP Berenika Peštová (ANO) pushed through a two-year transition period: goods placed on the market before September 27 are to be treated as compliant for another two years after the law takes effect. Flour, pasta, long-life milk, hygiene products and cosmetics were mentioned as examples.

The business argument, backed by the Czech Association of Trade and Tourism, has its economic logic. Repackaging long-life goods or pulling them from sale means huge logistics costs and waste. Retailers will ultimately pass those costs on in prices, and the customer pays again.

Yet the Ministry of Industry and Trade, which drafted the law, opposed the blanket delay. European supervisory authorities had already agreed to handle old stock case by case and not to force retailers to destroy goods merely because of an outdated label. Slovakia, unlike us, has managed perfectly well with this pragmatic approach.

So the Czech Republic has granted itself two years of blanket peace. Because the law is not yet in force, the period will in practice be somewhat longer. Experts warn that this version of the law will bring confusion to the market instead of relief, and potentially a penalty from the European Commission for incorrectly implementing the directive.

The law now sits with the Senate, which must decide on it by December 2. The senators face a clear question: will they put two years of comfort for companies ahead of consumers' right to truthful information?

Why it harms us

💸 Supporters of the transition period argue that the delay will not endanger consumers' lives or health. That is true; a misleading label poisons no one. But it takes away our ability to make purchases based on reality rather than on an engineered impression.

For two more years we will be paying for promises nobody has verified. A manufacturer who has genuinely invested in greener production and certification will stand on the shelf in the same row as a competitor who simply bought some green paint.

In such an environment, the "as if" green disguise wins and honesty loses. And all of us will pay that bill, even though the new law was meant to protect us.

How to undress the packaging

🔍 As long as Czech law stays silent, we have to rely on our own judgment. Three simple habits are enough to expose empty green promises:

  • Turn the product around. Marketing promises live on the front, where the selling happens. The back carries the ingredients and the details the manufacturer is legally liable for. If you find nothing on the back that supports the green claim on the front, you are buying only the color.
  • Finish the sentence on the label. Try completing it in your head: "Nature-friendly, because…" If the packaging offers no concrete continuation, you are paying for an empty phrase.
  • Look for a number, not a flower. The official European eco-label, EU Ecolabel, must display a licence number next to the logo, which you can look up in the European Commission's public catalogue. If a green logo has no verifiable number, you are looking at mere graphics, not a certificate.

Consumer protection today, paradoxically, depends on which bank of the Morava River you happen to stand on. On one side of the bridge, the green color must carry real proof. On the other, anyone who prints it may keep wearing it.

Green packaging never meant green inside. The only difference is whether the law watches out for you, or you have to figure it out yourself.
